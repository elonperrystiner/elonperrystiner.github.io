Storytelling
through technology.

I’m interested in how a story becomes something people can actually interact with, whether that’s a game, a learning experience, or an AI product.

The irony of working in spaces where I’m meant to show what’s possible with AI, while sending people to a Wix page, wasn’t lost on me. So I got to work.

I wanted to make my design thinking visible, including the versions that didn’t feel right and what I changed. I kept the prompts and the changes here because they say more about how I work than a finished screenshot does.

The Wix of it all

A little about me.

I’ve always been interested in telling stories, and in what happens when someone gets to be part of one. That’s taken me through children’s television at PBS KIDS, studying instructional technology and media at Teachers College, Columbia, and making games and other learning experiences.

I care about making things engaging and giving them a point of view. I also want to understand who they’re for. My NSF-supported voice research came out of questions about identity and belonging, and whether the technology could do a better job of reflecting the person using it.

I wanted to make my design thinking visible here, too. Rather than write a whole essay about how much I love iteration, I figured I could let you see me do it. So we’re starting with the Wix page.

Ideating and iterating

I was figuring out what I wanted while I was prompting and looking at the versions.

I liked the idea of a Pinterest board, with a grid and a collage happening together. I wanted the scraps to interact, rather than just be separate pictures sitting next to each other.

It took a while to explain what I meant. Some versions technically had those things, but they still looked generic to me.

The references helped me figure out what I was responding to. Then I kept adjusting.

I kept coming back to vintage clippings, photos, handwriting, and ephemera. I liked how a Pinterest board lets different things live together, and I wanted that feeling with brighter, saturated jewel tones.

The textures needed to be different, too. Not all scraps of paper with the same texture. I wanted it bright, saturated, a little surreal, and more personal than what I was getting back.

I liked the collage, but there was so much to do that it felt like a chore.

At one point, there were fourteen pieces and a character named Pip who could rephrase a message. I didn’t really know what Pip was supposed to be yet, and the interaction wasn’t doing anything that interesting.

I wanted to keep the collage, but give each project one clipping. Then you could open it and see the other images, instead of having everything competing for attention at once.

The colors were getting there, but I wanted it to feel like something.

I wanted different textures, not the same paper treatment on every scrap. And the purple background needed to feel crumpled, like one of many pieces of paper I’d filled with ideas.

The fonts and tiny text were bothering me, too. I wanted the annotations to feel handwritten, and the regular copy to be big enough that you’d actually want to read it.

Then the crumpled paper became the way into the site.

I pictured a trash can full of ideas, with one purple paper on top that seemed worth another look. You’d click it and it would unfold into the page.

At first it was too realistic. I wanted a doodle, like the annotations, so we took out the polished shading. Then I moved the purple one into the can, centered the whole thing, and gave it a purple glow and a little movement.

So, here’s where we landed.

I liked where the collage had landed. The conversation, the texture changes, and the paper idea had become part of how I got there, so I wanted you to be able to click through that, too.

And the work itself still needed to be easy to find. Each project has one clipping, with its images and videos inside. The entrance and the story are there if you want them; you can also go straight to the projects.

Even once the interactions worked, it still felt too generic. I went back through the copy and the way each piece was framed. I wanted the care I was putting into the decisions to show up in the page itself.

Things I’ve made.

Back to how this happened ↑How I got to this work ↓

I started out overseeing productions, and over time I wanted to make more of the pieces myself.

I’ve always cared about what makes someone want to stay with a story. The work has taken me from children’s television to learning products and AI, and each part has given me a little more of the process to get my hands on.

one thing led to another ↗
01 / PBS KIDS2021–2025
Senior Content Development Associate

At PBS KIDS, I was overseeing $5M+ productions and everything that had to happen around them.

I directed multiplatform properties funded through DOE Ready to Learn grants. That included creative development and audience research, but also the budgets, federal requirements, and competitive RFPs. I was responsible for keeping those pieces moving so the shows could get made and reach kids.

I also managed relationships across PBS, CPB, and 330+ member stations. The properties reached 13M+ monthly users, so there were a lot of people and requirements to account for. I liked being that involved in a production. I was also starting to wonder what it would be like to make more of the pieces myself.

02 / TEACHERS COLLEGE, COLUMBIAMA, Instructional Technology & Media
Completed May 2026

Then I went back to school and realized code wasn’t that scary.

At Teachers College, I started making things I would previously have assumed someone else needed to code. I could try an idea, see it on a screen, and keep changing it until it did more of what I had in mind.

They were small fish, but they were things I could actually use. That made me want to keep going.

03 / CERES, BECAUSE I WANTED TOA personal project, outside school and work

Ceres was something I wanted to make because it seemed fun.

My best friend is also my personal astrologer, so when he had an idea for a pocket astrologer, I was interested. It wasn’t schoolwork or work-work. I could spend time thinking about what would make it fun to use and feel like its own thing.

He led the programming. I designed the brand and interfaces and worked out features like Decision Windows and Field Notes. I also thought through how a question in the chat should lead to the right astrological tools, without requiring everyone to already know what to ask for.

04 / VOICE & IDENTITYNSF GRFP-supported graduate research

The voice research made me think harder about who I was designing for.

For my NSF-supported research, I started with my own voice and the ways it changes depending on who I’m talking to. I wanted to know how much of that an AI voice tool could understand, and what would get lost.

I tested variations in ElevenLabs, developed a four-category acoustic profile, and used what I was finding to redesign the onboarding. I wanted someone to be able to describe themselves before having to choose a voice. It brought the question of belonging into the actual design decisions.

05 / THE EMANCIPATION GAME2026–present
Chief of Staff, previously Product Manager & Learning Designer

The game was the big fish. I was making much more of it myself, and managing what it took to get it built.

I serve as Chief of Staff to the CEO and lead product management and learning design for The Emancipation Game. I got involved in what students would do in the game, how the characters would respond, and what teachers would need to use it in a classroom.

I wrote scripts and dialogue, designed missions and assessments, and made visual and audio assets. In Convai and ElevenLabs, I worked on what the characters said and how they sounded. I also tested and revised the prompts behind AI scoring and student feedback.

When school partners gave us feedback, I worked out what needed to change and wrote the creative and technical specifications for developers. I put together the classroom implementation plan and designed and led AI Praxis training around the problems partners were running into.

I was managing developers, specialists, vendors, and QA alongside that work. So I was making individual pieces while also keeping track of what everyone else needed to get theirs done.

This one really was a labor of love. ↗

I’d wanted to get more hands-on, and the game gave me plenty of opportunities to find out what that meant. I had to think through how a script would become a mission, what would happen after a student answered, and when they should get another try or more help. I tested prompts and put those decisions into UX specifications and standards-linked assessment requirements.

I also designed the UI and functional specifications for the prompt-management dashboard and specified what we needed from gameplay analytics and exports. Engineers built the systems. Technical specialists wrote some of the backend and scoring specifications under my direction, and I oversee the expert-led research for the RAG knowledge base.

The product was scoped for 900 students across four partner sites. I wrote teacher guidance and training and adjusted the implementation plan when school schedules changed. I had to think about what would happen when a teacher was using this and we weren’t there to explain it.

That’s why this project means so much to me. I got to write the story and make assets, but I also had to take responsibility for how the work would get built and used. It gave me a chance to find out whether I could do the kind of work I’d been moving toward.

06 / THE NEXT CHAPTERA few more things worth making.

There are still a lot of things I want to make.

I want to keep working on things that make people curious enough to stick around. Games and learning experiences are a big part of that for me, and I’m interested in what AI makes possible when there’s a real reason to use it.

I like having a hand in the idea, getting into the details, and seeing what happens when someone actually uses it. I’d like to keep getting better at all of that.
